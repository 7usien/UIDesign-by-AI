# Creative Direction

## Form one thesis

Start with one sentence: `Reading this as: <page kind> for <audience>, where <subject-specific visual world> makes <desired outcome> credible.`

Write a concise internal direction before implementation:

- **Positioning idea:** the belief the page should make credible.
- **Narrative:** how attention moves from problem to proof to action.
- **Signature mechanism:** one memorable visual behavior tied to the product or service.
- **Composition:** grid, density, rhythm, scale, and intentional disruption.
- **Typography:** functional roles and why their contrast fits the audience.
- **Media:** what carries proof and what is deliberately absent.
- **Motion:** what changes, why it changes, and reduced-motion behavior.
- **Control levels:** composition variance, motion intensity, and information density on a 1–10 scale, with a reason for each value.

Do not present generic labels such as “premium editorial,” “modern minimal,” or “dark luxury” as a direction.

## Pass the substitution test

Imagine replacing the logo and nouns with those of an unrelated business. If the same layout, visuals, and section logic still work, the direction is too generic. Reconnect at least the signature mechanism, proof format, and composition to the actual offer.

## Control the visual system

- Use one dominant idea per viewport.
- Create meaningful contrast in scale, density, pace, or material—not random variation.
- Choose type for content character and language performance, not trend recognition.
- Use accent color to signal hierarchy or meaning.
- Allow visual richness when the concept earns it; restraint does not mean blandness.
- Avoid designing every section as an isolated card or centered headline block.
- Give each section family a distinct job. Repeating the same split layout, equal-card grid, eyebrow, or reveal rhythm is a template signal unless the repetition communicates a real sequence.
- Let the subject supply the visual vocabulary: its objects, interfaces, materials, data, language, environment, or process. Do not import a generic “luxury” or “tech” vocabulary.
- Take one defensible aesthetic risk. It must strengthen the thesis without weakening comprehension, accessibility, or conversion.

The goal is coherent surprise: visually memorable, immediately understandable, and credible to the intended buyer.
